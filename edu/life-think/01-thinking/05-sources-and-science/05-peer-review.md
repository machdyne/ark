# Peer review and one study

```lesson
(objective "Explain peer review, and why one study is not the last word.")
(minutes 20)
(keywords "peer review" "replication")
```

You can explain how science checks its own work, and why you should
wait for more than one study.

Before most scientific journals publish a study, they send it to other
experts in the field. They read it and look for mistakes, weak
methods or claims that go beyond the results. This is **peer review**
("peers" are equals).

Peer review catches many problems, but not all. Reviewers usually do
not repeat the experiment themselves.

```sources
(source "Understanding Science, University of California Museum of Paleontology")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "What does peer review do?")
  (right "Other experts check a study for problems before it is published")
  (wrong "It proves the study is right"
    "Reviewers can miss mistakes, and they rarely redo the work. Peer review lowers the risk of error; it does not remove it.")
  (wrong "The public votes on the study"
    "Peer review is done by experts in the field, not by a vote.")
  (hint "Who are a scientist's peers?"))
```

---

## Replication

**Replication** means doing a study again, often by a different team,
to see if the same result comes out. A result that is replicated many
times is much more trustworthy than one that has been found once.

One study can be wrong by chance, from a small sample, or from a
mistake. That is why scientists talk about the weight of evidence
from many studies.

```check
(choice
  (ask "A single new study reports a surprising result. What is the most reasonable view?")
  (right "Interesting, but wait to see if other studies find the same")
  (wrong "It is published, so it must be true"
    "Even peer-reviewed studies can be wrong. A surprising result needs replication.")
  (wrong "It goes against what was known, so it must be false"
    "Science does change with new evidence. The right response is to wait for more studies, not to reject it outright.")
  (hint "What makes a result trustworthy?"))
```

---

## Science changes its mind

When new evidence is strong and replicated, scientists change their
view. That is a strength, not a weakness: science is a way of finding
and fixing mistakes. When most experts in a field agree after many
studies, that agreement is the best guide available.

```check
(choice
  (ask "\"Scientists changed their advice, so science can't be trusted.\" What is wrong with this?")
  (right "Changing a view when new evidence comes in is how science corrects itself")
  (wrong "Nothing: if advice changes, it was never worth anything"
    "That would mean any view that ever improves was worthless. Science's ability to correct itself is why it is reliable over time.")
  (hint "Is changing your mind on new evidence a flaw?"))
```

## Worked example

```example
(example
  (problem "A made-up headline: \"New study: chewing gum makes you smarter!\" How should you think about it?")
  (step "One study: has it been replicated?")
  (step "Was it peer-reviewed? Read laterally: what do other experts say?")
  (step "Is the headline secondary? Check what the study itself measured.")
  (answer "Treat it as a possible finding, not a fact, until more studies agree."))
```

## Practice

```check
(choice
  (ask "Which result is most trustworthy?")
  (right "One found by many teams in many studies")
  (wrong "One from a single large study reported widely in the news"
    "Size and fame help less than repetition. A result found again and again by different teams is stronger.")
  (wrong "One a famous scientist believes strongly"
    "Belief is not evidence. That is an appeal to authority if the studies do not back it.")
  (hint "Think replication."))
```

So: peer review checks a study, replication tests it again, and many
studies outweigh one.
