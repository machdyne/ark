# Ways to test yourself

```lesson
(objective "Use flashcards, a blank page and practice questions to test yourself, checking each answer.")
(minutes 20)
(keywords "flashcard" "free recall")
```

You can test yourself in many ways, with nothing more than paper and a
pen. Here are 3 that work, and one rule for all of them.

A **flashcard** is a small card with a question or a word on the front
and the answer on the back. To use one, read the front and give the
answer, out loud or on paper. Only then turn it over and check.

Saying "I know this one" and flipping it is not enough. Give the whole
answer first.

```sources
(source "Dunlosky and others, Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 2013: practice testing")
(source "Brown, Roediger and McDaniel, Make It Stick (Harvard University Press, 2014), chapter 2")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Kim looks at a flashcard, thinks \"I know that\", and turns it over. What should she do instead?")
  (right "Say or write the full answer before she turns it over")
  (wrong "Turn it over faster, to get through more cards"
    "Going faster without answering skips the part that helps. The memory gets stronger when she actually pulls the answer out.")
  (wrong "Nothing: thinking she knows it is the same thing"
    "Feeling that you know is not the same as being able to say it. That is the illusion of knowing.")
  (hint "What makes it retrieval practice and not just looking?"))
```

## A blank page

Take a blank page. Write the name of a topic at the top. Then write
down everything you remember about it, without looking at anything.

Writing all you can remember, in any order, is called **free recall**.
It shows you what you know and what is missing. When you run out, open
your notes and add what you missed, in another color.

```sources
(source "Roediger and Karpicke, Test-Enhanced Learning, Psychological Science 17(3), 2006")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "After a free recall page, what should you do with the parts you missed?")
  (right "Check your notes and add them, then try recalling them again later")
  (wrong "Nothing: the page was only to see what you know"
    "Seeing what is missing is half of it. The gaps tell you exactly what to study, and to test again later.")
  (wrong "Start again by rereading the whole chapter"
    "You already know which parts you missed. Going back to just those is quicker and works better.")
  (hint "What does the blank page show you that rereading does not?"))
```

## Practice questions

Questions are everywhere: at the end of a chapter, on old quizzes, in
learn. You can also write your own. Turn each heading in your notes
into a question, and answer it later without looking.

```check
(choice
  (ask "A heading in Omar's notes says \"Why the Moon has phases\". Which question can he make from it?")
  (right "Why does the Moon seem to change shape during a month?")
  (wrong "Is the Moon interesting?"
    "That question has no answer to check. A good question asks for the idea under the heading.")
  (wrong "What page is the Moon on?"
    "That asks where the idea is, not what it is. Ask for the idea itself, so you have to recall it.")
  (hint "Turn the heading itself into a question with a real answer."))
```

---

## The rule: check every answer

Testing yourself helps most when you then check whether you were right.
If you were wrong, you find out what is right, and you do not keep a
wrong idea.

```example
(example
  (problem "You have 12 new words to learn for Spanish class.")
  (step "Make a flashcard for each word: the English word on the front, the Spanish on the back.")
  (step "Go through the pile. Say each answer, then check it.")
  (step "Put the cards you got right on one side. Go through the ones you missed again.")
  (step "Keep going until you have got each card right once.")
  (answer "Then put them away, and test yourself on all 12 again in a day or two."))
```

```sources
(source "Dunlosky and others, Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 2013: practice testing")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Why check each answer after you test yourself?")
  (right "So a wrong answer gets fixed instead of being practiced")
  (wrong "Checking does not matter; only the testing does"
    "Testing helps even without checking. But if you are wrong and never find out, you may keep the wrong answer.")
  (wrong "To count your score"
    "A score can be nice to know, but the point is to fix what you got wrong.")
  (hint "What happens to a wrong answer that nobody notices?"))
```

## Practice

```check
(choice
  (ask "Which of these is free recall?")
  (right "Writing everything you remember about volcanoes on a blank page")
  (wrong "Matching words to their meanings on a worksheet"
    "A worksheet like that shows you the answers to pick from. Free recall starts from nothing but your memory.")
  (wrong "Reading your notes on volcanoes out loud"
    "Reading out loud is still reading. Free recall means writing it without looking.")
  (hint "Free recall starts with a blank page."))
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Which flashcard habit is best?")
  (right "Keep testing yourself on the cards you missed until you get them right")
  (wrong "Take out the cards you missed, because they are too hard"
    "The cards you miss are the ones you most need. Put them back in, not out.")
  (wrong "Look at the back first, so you learn the answer"
    "Looking first turns the card into reading. Try to answer, then look.")
  (hint "Which cards need the most practice?"))
```

## This week

Make 10 flashcards for something you are learning now. Test yourself
on them 3 times this week, saying each answer before you turn the card.

So: flashcards, a blank page and practice questions all make you
recall, and checking each answer fixes your mistakes.
