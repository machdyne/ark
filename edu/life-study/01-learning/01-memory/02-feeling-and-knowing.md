# Feeling and knowing

```lesson
(objective "Explain why rereading and highlighting feel like learning but help less than they seem to.")
(minutes 15)
(keywords "illusion of knowing")
```

Reading your notes again and highlighting them feel like learning, but
they help much less than they seem to. Many students study mostly
this way.

When you read a page a second time, the words look familiar. Reading
feels smooth and easy, and that feels like knowing. But seeing that
something is familiar is not the same as being able to recall it on
your own.

The feeling that you know something because it looks familiar, when you
could not recall it without the page, is an **illusion of knowing**.

```sources
(source "Brown, Roediger and McDaniel, Make It Stick (Harvard University Press, 2014), chapters 1 and 5")
(source "Dunlosky and others, Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 2013: rereading; highlighting")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Sam reads his science notes 3 times. They look very familiar now. What does that tell him?")
  (right "Only that he would know them if he saw them, not that he can recall them")
  (wrong "That he knows them well enough for the test"
    "Familiar is not the same as remembered. On a test, the page is not there to look at, so he needs to recall it himself.")
  (wrong "That rereading is the best way to study"
    "Rereading makes things look familiar, which feels good. That feeling can fool you into thinking you know more than you do.")
  (hint "On a test, is the page in front of you?"))
```

## Feeling sure, and being right

In one experiment, college students read a short text. Some read it 4
times. Others read it once and then wrote down all they could
remember, 3 times.

The students who only read it felt surest they would remember it.
But a week later, the students who had written down what they
remembered recalled more of it.

```sources
(source "Roediger and Karpicke, Test-Enhanced Learning, Psychological Science 17(3), 2006, experiment 2")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "What does this experiment show about the feeling of knowing?")
  (right "Feeling sure you will remember is not a good guide to what you will remember")
  (wrong "Reading 4 times works best, because those students felt surest"
    "They felt surest, but they remembered less a week later. The feeling misled them.")
  (wrong "Nobody remembers anything after a week"
    "Both groups remembered some of the text. The students who had practiced recalling it remembered more.")
  (hint "Compare who felt surest with who remembered more."))
```

## What the research says

In 2013, 5 researchers looked at the studies of 10 ways of studying. They rated each one by how useful it is.

Rereading and highlighting were both rated **low**: they help a little,
or not at all, compared with better ways. Testing yourself and
spreading your study out were rated **high**. You learn both in this
course.

```sources
(source "Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham, Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 2013")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Which 2 ways of studying did the review rate as most useful?")
  (right "Testing yourself, and spreading your study out")
  (wrong "Rereading, and highlighting"
    "Those are popular, but the review rated both low. They feel useful because the page looks familiar.")
  (wrong "Highlighting, and testing yourself"
    "Testing yourself was rated high, but highlighting was rated low. Marking a word is not the same as recalling it.")
  (hint "The lesson said you learn both of them in this course."))
```

## A quick check on yourself

You can catch the illusion of knowing with one simple move: close the
book and try.

```example
(example
  (problem "You have read a page about the water cycle. Do you know it?")
  (step "Close the book.")
  (step "Say or write the main ideas from memory: what happens to the water, and in what order.")
  (step "Open the book and check. Mark what you missed or got wrong.")
  (answer "What you could recall, you know for now. What you missed is what to study next."))
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Ana has read a page twice. What is the best next step?")
  (right "Close the book and try to write the main ideas")
  (wrong "Read it a third time, more slowly"
    "A third reading will feel even more familiar, but it will not tell her what she can recall.")
  (wrong "Highlight the important sentences"
    "Highlighting picks out sentences, but she still has not tried to recall anything on her own.")
  (hint "What is the one move that catches the illusion of knowing?"))
```

## Practice

```check
(choice
  (ask "Why does rereading feel like it works?")
  (right "The words look familiar, and familiar feels like knowing")
  (wrong "Because it really is the best way to learn"
    "The research rated rereading low. It feels good because reading familiar words is smooth and easy.")
  (wrong "Because it takes a long time"
    "Time spent is not the same as learning. What matters is what you can recall later.")
  (hint "What happens to the words the second time you read them?"))
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Which of these catches an illusion of knowing?")
  (right "Covering the answers and trying to give them")
  (wrong "Underlining the key words in a new color"
    "Underlining still has the page in front of you. You only find out what you know when you try without it.")
  (wrong "Reading the summary at the end of the chapter"
    "A summary is still reading. It will feel familiar, which is the illusion, not the check.")
  (hint "The check is to try without the page."))
```

## This week

The next time you want to reread something, close it first. Write down
3 things you remember, then check them.

So: familiar is not the same as known, and the way to tell is to try
without the page.
