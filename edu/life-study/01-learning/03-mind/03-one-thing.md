# One thing at a time

```lesson
(objective "Explain why doing two things at once makes your work slower and less accurate.")
(minutes 15)
(keywords "multitasking" "switch cost")
```

You work faster and make fewer mistakes when you do one thing at a
time.

Trying to do 2 or more things at once, like homework while chatting
with friends on a phone, is called **multitasking**. It can feel like
doing both at once. But when 2 tasks both need you to think, your mind
mostly switches back and forth between them.

Every switch costs something. The time lost, and the extra mistakes,
when you change from one task to another are called a
**switch cost**. Experiments find switch costs even when people know
the switch is coming.

```sources
(source "Monsell, Task Switching, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 7(3), 2003")
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "When you do homework while chatting, what is your mind mostly doing?")
  (right "Switching back and forth between the two")
  (wrong "Doing both fully at the same time"
    "It can feel that way, but when both tasks need thinking, the mind mostly switches between them, and each switch costs something.")
  (wrong "Getting better at both, because it is working harder"
    "Switching does not train you to do both well. Each switch costs time and adds mistakes.")
  (hint "What is a switch cost?"))
```

## Small costs add up

One switch costs only a little. But a message every few minutes means
many switches in an hour, and each one pulls you away from what you
were doing.

```check
(number
  (ask "Ella checks her phone 4 times every 15 minutes while she studies for 1 hour. How many switches away from her work is that?")
  (answer 16) (unit "switches") (from "4 * 4")
  (wrong-if 4 "That is in 15 minutes. An hour has 4 lots of 15 minutes.")
  (wrong-if 60 "That counts minutes, not switches. She switches 4 times in each 15 minutes.")
  (hint "How many 15-minute parts are in 1 hour?")
  (hint "4 parts, with 4 switches in each."))
```

## What to do

These are simple habits, not research results, but they cut down
switches:

- Put your phone in another room, or turn off its sounds while you
  study.
- Close everything on the screen that is not your task.
- Work for a set time, then take a short break, and check messages
  then.

```example
(example
  (problem "Finn has 30 minutes of math homework and keeps getting messages.")
  (step "He puts his phone in the kitchen, with its sounds off.")
  (step "He works on the math for 25 minutes, one problem at a time.")
  (step "Then he takes a 5-minute break and reads his messages.")
  (answer "One task at a time, and messages saved for the break."))
```

```check
(choice
  (ask "Which plan has the fewest switches?")
  (right "Phone in another room, work for 25 minutes, then a break")
  (wrong "Phone on the desk, but only answer the important messages"
    "Each message still pulls you away to decide if it is important. That is a switch too.")
  (wrong "Music videos playing beside the homework, to stay relaxed"
    "Videos keep pulling your eyes and mind away. Each glance is a small switch.")
  (hint "Which plan keeps the other things out of reach?"))
```

## How learn does it

learn has no streaks, points, reminders or notifications. It shows one
step at a time, and nothing else competes for the screen.

```check
(choice
  (ask "Why does learn show one step at a time, with no reminders or notifications?")
  (right "So nothing pulls your attention away from what you are learning")
  (wrong "Because it cannot show more than one thing"
    "learn can show text and a figure together. Leaving out reminders and alerts is on purpose.")
  (wrong "To make the lessons take longer"
    "Leaving distractions out does not make a lesson longer. Each one would only cost you a switch.")
  (hint "What does a notification do to your attention?"))
```
